01 / RESEARCH MOTIVATION

Can we identify a victim without knowing its name?

Underground cybercrime ecosystems contain intelligence that can precede public disclosure of a cyberattack. Initial access brokers, ransomware operators and data sellers sometimes describe their targets through industry, geography, revenue and technological characteristics while deliberately withholding the organization’s name. For defenders, this creates a gap between observing a threat and knowing whom to warn.

Existing darknet analysis methods frequently focus on classifying illicit activity or extracting explicitly named entities. Neither task directly resolves an identity that is absent from the text. This research approaches the problem as open-world victim inference: can a language model generate a useful shortlist of real organizations from partial corporate attributes, without a predefined candidate database?

02 / METHODOLOGY

From raw intelligence to ranked hypotheses

The framework separates the extraction of attributes, experimental evaluation of candidate generation, and external corroboration. Its central experiment measures the knowledge already internalized by language models, rather than their ability to retrieve answers from the web.

Select a stage to explore the research workflow.

Experimental design

A corpus of 55 real-world underground postings informed the attribute schema. A separate ground-truth dataset was constructed from 100 named victim cases, enriched with corporate attributes and subsequently anonymized. Each model received five fields: country, sector, revenue, employee count and headquarters. The organization name was withheld.

Seven models spanning large cloud, small cloud and local deployment tiers were evaluated under the same prompt design, without web search or retrieval augmentation. Each query requested five ranked candidates, and eight independent runs were performed for every case and model. Company-name variants were normalized before aggregation.

Rank aggregation and evaluation

Borda count assigns five points to a first-ranked candidate, four to second place, and so on down to one point for fifth place. Scores are summed across the eight runs and used to produce a consolidated ranking. The original experiment also retained repeated nominations within an individual response when assigning points.

Hit Rate at K (HR@K) measures the proportion of cases in which the correct organization appears within the first K positions. Mean Rank measures its average position among successful matches. Together they distinguish coverage from the practical effort required to inspect a shortlist.

03 / EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

What the models actually know

GPT-4.1 achieved the highest hit rate, identifying the correct company within its top five consolidated candidates in 43 out of 100 cases. It placed the correct company first in 33 cases. This is a meaningful baseline for an open-world task, but it is not a reliable confirmation of victim identity.

Company size is a major constraint

Performance was systematically higher for large organizations. The dataset contained 78 large, 16 medium and six small companies, grouped by the revenue thresholds used in the thesis. Six of the seven models failed to identify any small company; GPT-4.1 identified one of six. For large companies, its HR@5 reached 48.7%. Most models clustered around 25% for medium companies.

This pattern is consistent with unequal organizational visibility in pre-training data, although the experiment does not directly inspect those corpora. The small-company sample is also limited, so its results should not be generalized without further evaluation.

More metadata is not always better

A leave-one-out ablation study removed each of the five attributes in turn. Paired outcomes were evaluated using McNemar’s test. Sector was the strongest and most consistent signal: removing it reduced total match rate by 19.9 percentage points on average, with statistically significant drops for all seven models (p < 0.001).

Headquarters provided additional information for some models, while country offered little incremental value when other attributes were present. Revenue and employee count sometimes introduced noise: GPT-4.1 improved by six percentage points in total match rate when either field was omitted separately (p = 0.016 in each condition). Differences in reporting periods, provider estimates and sector classifications can materially affect the input profile.

Prediction stability matters

Repeated queries revealed a marked distinction between cloud models and local open-weight models. Local models exhibited substantially greater variation in the candidates returned across eight iterations. Ranked aggregation therefore plays an important role in extracting consistent signals from variable outputs. A stable prediction, however, is not necessarily a correct prediction.

04 / OPERATIONAL IMPLICATIONS

From hypotheses to evidence

The research proposes an external intelligence layer that complements attribute-only candidate generation. Historical incident reporting, public infrastructure observations, certificate transparency information and authorized commercial intelligence can contribute contextual evidence during defensive analyst review.

These signals are intended to help analysts assess competing hypotheses, not to establish compromise on their own. A historical incident or technology match does not prove that an organization is the victim described in a new posting. The thesis describes the validation workflow but does not report a controlled quantitative improvement in hit rate attributable to this stage.

Responsible use

Candidate rankings are investigative leads, not confirmed identities. Use only authorized intelligence sources, avoid publishing speculative victim identities, and independently corroborate evidence before any notification or operational action.

05 / LIMITATIONS & FUTURE WORK

A baseline, not a finished attribution system

Even the strongest model missed more than half the ground-truth cases within its top five candidates. The dataset is skewed toward large companies, and its corporate metadata is affected by inconsistent business intelligence classifications and estimates. Enriched historical victim profiles also differ from incomplete, noisy live underground postings. The reported results therefore describe the experimental benchmark, not measured accuracy on live threats.

The zero-shot design deliberately excludes retrieval, so it cannot resolve knowledge gaps through up-to-date corporate sources. Future research could investigate retrieval-augmented generation, domain-specific fine-tuning, empirically weighted attributes and independently evaluated validation workflows. Larger and more representative datasets would help establish how well the findings generalize.
